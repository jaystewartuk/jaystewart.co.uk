Case study

SpiralClass

A scheduling, payments and video platform for independent teachers. Teachers publish a booking page, sell class packages, meet students in the browser and share materials afterwards.

Role
Product, design, engineering and operation, solo, with coding agents under review
Status
Live One teacher and her eight students; real payments
Stack
TypeScript, Next.js, Postgres and Prisma, Stripe Connect, Wise, self-hosted LiveKit video
Links
spiralclass.com · demo · source

Let the teacher own the method

An early teacher was developing a structured method for her language school. The existing AI lesson writer produced a generic lesson shape, which would leave her reworking drafts to match the sequence she already used.

I treated her method as teacher-owned material, not a curriculum for the platform to publish for everyone. Teachers can write reusable lesson templates; the AI composer can use a selected template as its section structure, then adapt the content to the student's level and goals.

This used the template editor and generation flow already in the product. The structure is optional, so teachers who do not use templates keep the existing generation path. It also keeps the feature useful beyond language teaching: each teacher supplies their own method.

Make payments fit the teacher's country

The first card-payment design collected a student's money on the platform and transferred it to the teacher. That depended on Stripe's cross-border payout rules and excluded the launch market. It also put the platform in the middle of disputes and made the buyer's available payment methods depend on the platform's country.

I changed the funds flow: Stripe charges on the teacher's own connected account, and pays her locally. The platform does not hold or forward that money. Wise remains a separate option for teachers outside Stripe's measured coverage.

Testing a real teacher's checkout surfaced a less obvious issue. Her account reported its local payment capabilities as active, but checkout still offered cards only. The connected account had inherited a parent payment-method configuration where local methods were off. I traced the gap to the Stripe dashboard configuration, enabled the methods there, and verified that OXXO and SPEI appeared in checkout. A green capability flag alone had not proved the buyer's experience.

Build for the full class lifecycle

The product connects the work teachers otherwise split across tools: availability and booking, package balances, payment reconciliation, browser video, homework and materials. That makes correctness at the boundaries matter. A cancelled class has to restore or forfeit the right credit; a payment has to reconcile to the right student and package; a student should only see their own teacher's content.

I kept those rules in domain modules with integration coverage against a real database, and recorded product and architecture decisions as they changed. The repository contains nearly 150 decision records, including reversals that explain what new evidence changed the earlier choice.

What the evidence says so far

SpiralClass is live with one teacher and her eight students, real payments have gone through it, and I operate it as its only maintainer. That is evidence of a running product and real workflows, not evidence of scale. I do not have enough payment or feature-usage data to claim that the checkout changes improved conversion or that the template composer is widely used. The next decisions should follow what teachers and students actually use and where their work still gets stuck.
